From Crying in My Third Year to an Amazon Return Offer

Career Reflection Amazon Internship OMSCS

Where I Started

Going into my fourth year of college, I had nothing lined up. FAANG was something I dreamed about but never thought was real.

I remember crying and having a breakdown with my girlfriend. I asked why I was born into this life and why everything I did felt like a waste of time. School, work, all of it felt like it wasn't going my way. I was about to quit the path as a SWE because I didn't see the point anymore.

I had lost sight of why I liked coding in the first place. It felt joyless. I felt like I had forgotten how to code, the rejections didn't help, and watching people my age succeed hurt. I felt behind and jealous, and I felt like the worst one in the classroom. Looking back, that was imposter syndrome, but at the time it just felt like something was wrong with me.

The Bot That Brought Back the Fun

My good friend Alan (nickname "tu papa"), who I met through someone from an FC Mobile game, inspired me to start building a gaming community with him. For free. He told me to just let my mind do the magic.

That became the Valhalla FC bot, for our EA Sports FC Mobile community, and it was the turning point of my career. I built it for fun. It combined coding and FC Mobile, two things I loved, and it was actually useful to people. That brought back the fun I had lost.

Summer 2024 felt like an unpaid internship by itself. I'd get an idea, figure out how I could make it work, deep dive into it, and seeing things work was fun. I wasn't expecting much from it, but it was the start of something. After the bot came out, everyone in the FC Mobile community tried to copy it, and I was the only one left standing. Being copied felt validating, because it meant people were actually using my work.

The Fifth Year I Thought Was Dumb

My girlfriend saw something in me that I had lost track of. She pushed me to take a path others wouldn't: stay a fifth year at UCSD for one more shot at internships.

At the time I thought it was a dumb idea. I figured one year couldn't make a difference. I was wrong.

UCSD Health and Office Hours

In my fourth year, my bot experience (databases and some HTML) landed me my first campus job at UCSD Health as a software dev. It was $24.50 an hour, 20 hours a week, and I was really happy. I learned the basics of software, how to maintain it, and how to work on a team.

In the interview I was surprised that I knew how to answer the questions on the spot even though I didn't feel ready. The answers came naturally. Later I became the main person on a project, and in one hour-long design discussion I could think of everything quickly and answer the roadblocks that came up. Database design came up in that conversation, and that thread comes back later in this post.

I worked on a lot of projects there and picked up a lot about databases. I call it my one year of experience. Features I built saved the team about $10k to $15k a year.

That year I also started going to office hours for my CSE 127 projects. Mostly because I liked the TA, who was chill and explained things really well. Going there made me realize I wasn't the dumbest one in the room. That was me overthinking, and a lot of people were just like me. It was the motivation and shift I needed.

My First Internship at Cbord

Then came my first internship, a summer at Cbord, a small tech company. I was also working on an AWS cloud certification at the time.

I didn't think I'd get it. The interviewer asked what OOP was and I blanked on the term, though I remembered some of the concepts. When I described my project, they asked how big the team was. I said one, and they looked shocked. I walked out sure it went badly because the vibes felt off.

It took 2 to 3 weeks to hear back, and I missed the first call. When I called back I was jumping up and down, probably hurting the HR person's ears. They told me it made their day and that this is why they do this job. I told my girlfriend first, then my parents.

At Cbord my manager Nathan pushed me every single day to do more and learn more. That internship showed me this path was right and I needed to keep grinding. Weekly one-on-ones were one of the things I looked forward to most. I'd ask Nathan about his career path and how he got where he is. I also built an AI chatbot there and loved making it. The best part was the reaction afterward, getting encouragement from people who had been in the field 5 to 20 years. It was a breath of fresh air. (I wrote a longer post about that summer.)

James and the LeetCode Grind

In fall 2025, James entered the picture. Within 3 to 4 months he became one of my closest friends. He had just finished an internship at Salesforce, was doing Georgia Tech online (OMSCS), was in San Diego, and was grinding LeetCode. At first I saw him as a gym buddy. We were pushing for the same goal, and he introduced me to NeetCode and LeetCode, which started my grind.

Our days looked like this: gym in the morning, campus for classes, then LeetCode and classwork together. About 24/7, 4 to 5 days a week, and we never got sick of each other. We prepped each other for interviews, applied to jobs, and did online assessments together. All of it was part of the experience.

My first big SDE interview was Capital One, at 5 am because of Eastern time. Round one was coding, pretty easy, but I felt the interviewer didn't like me. Round two was behavioral. Round three was a case study, the worst one, and a virtual card question messed me up. Again, that interviewer didn't seem to like me.

I got rejected shortly after. Instead of being sad, I kept grinding LeetCode. Something snapped in me. This is just part of it, and I had bigger fish to fry.

When James Left

Then James got into Google and moved to the Bay Area to start. I was proud of him, and also sad. I had no interviews lined up at that point. Instead of spiraling, I applied to Georgia Tech's online master's program (OMSCS) and got in within a week. James and my girlfriend were the push: just apply, what do you have to lose? I figured I might as well do something for the next 1.5 to 2 years instead of having no job.

Around then I also applied to the Amazon 2026 internship without thinking much of it. I was very consistent with LeetCode, 1 to 4 questions a day, making sure I understood everything and staying on top of coursework.

The online assessment was fairly easy, but I didn't do well on the second coding question and didn't think I'd pass. I got the email saying I passed the screening and OA while I was at the gym. I was on a call with my girlfriend and yelled WTF.

Within a month I had the interview with Shen, who became my manager. I was locked in on LeetCode and behavioral questions that month, without James nearby since he was already at Google. In the interview I talked about UCSD Health and the $10k to $15k a year in savings. I thought it went badly again because I couldn't answer a hashing question (the hashmap and array one).

One business day after the interview, I got the email saying I got the job. My reaction was "holy f*** I got into FAANG."

Looking at that table now, I misread the reactions at Cbord and Amazon. Capital One I read right, and that one did end in a rejection.

Summer at AWS DocumentDB

I spent summer 2026 in Seattle on the AWS DocumentDB team, a 12-week internship, and I was the only intern on my team. Databases again.

My first project was a full-stack internal tool in TypeScript and Python that automates the certification process for instances. It cut certification time by 30%. It also has an AI analysis component that studies failing certification tests and recommends next steps.

I finished early, so my manager Shen gave me a second project: an AI analyzer for a metrics pipeline, with live refresh and automated error alerting. It monitors all 50 of the team's pipelines.

The Return Offer

The internship ended in September 2026. On September 17, Amazon University Talent Acquisition sent an email saying I'd make an excellent Amazonian, but the org was still reviewing headcount. That meant no return offer yet, and no guarantee or timeline. I could possibly be placed on another team at the same role and level without re-interviewing. My manager had told me I would get the return offer.

Then the written offer arrived: SDE1 at AWS in Seattle, starting February 1, 2027. I accepted.

Where That Leaves Me

In my third year I was crying and asking why I was born into this life. Now there's an offer email in my inbox. The fifth year I thought was a dumb idea is part of how I got here.

Thank you to my girlfriend, who saw something in me when I couldn't. To Alan, for the bot. To James, for the grind. To Nathan and Shen, who pushed me. And to the TA who made office hours worth showing up to.
